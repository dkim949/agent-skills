# Report structure and review

Shape the report's argument and quality standard. Leave research, analysis, and file production to the relevant domain and artifact skills.

## Define the reporting job

Establish before drafting:

- primary reader and what they need to decide, understand, or verify
- central question, scope, time period, and comparison basis
- available evidence and material gaps
- requested artifact, template, language, length, and citation style
- publication venue or reporting guideline when applicable

Preserve the user's template and constraints. Do not imitate a specific consultancy's brand, proprietary method names, template, logo, or visual identity.

When paired with a report-building or artifact skill, follow its required audience, structure, and delivery contract; apply only compatible guidance from this skill.

## Choose the mode

Choose the report's primary mode from its purpose, not its filename.

- **Consulting:** the reader must make or approve a decision. Read `## Consulting mode` in [reporting-standards.md](reporting-standards.md).
- **Academic:** the reader must assess methods, validity, or reproducibility. Read `## Academic mode` in [reporting-standards.md](reporting-standards.md).
- **Hybrid:** a technical decision report needs an answer-first reading path and academic evidence discipline. Read both sections, but do not duplicate summaries or methods.

If the report contains charts or designed tables, also read `## Visual communication` in the reference.

## Build the report spine

Draft a short content plan before prose:

1. State the governing answer, recommendation, or main result in one or two sentences.
2. Select only the sections needed to support or qualify it.
3. Give each section one job and, where venue conventions allow, a heading that communicates its point. Otherwise state the takeaway in the opening sentence.
4. Within each section, connect claim, evidence, interpretation, and either implication or limitation.
5. End with the decision, next step, research implication, or unresolved question appropriate to the mode.

Use a familiar structure only when it serves the reader. Do not force an executive summary, IMRaD, KPI strip, or recommendation section onto every report.

## Protect evidence quality

- Trace material facts, numbers, charts, and derived values to their sources.
- Define metrics, units, cohorts, denominators, filters, time windows, and baselines before they carry an argument.
- Separate observation, calculation, inference, and recommendation when the distinction matters.
- Distinguish descriptive, predictive, inferential, and causal claims.
- Put uncertainty and limitations next to the conclusion they can change.
- Never invent evidence, citations, precision, or methodological detail to complete a preferred format.

## Keep the design functional

- Use visual hierarchy to expose the argument, not to decorate it.
- Choose charts from the analytical question and data relationship; use tables for exact lookup or audit detail.
- Keep actual, target, forecast, benchmark, and scenario notation consistent across the report.
- Pair every material visual with a nearby textual takeaway and accessible equivalent.
- Prefer a clear top-to-bottom reading path unless the user requests a dashboard or presentation layout.

## Review before delivery

Confirm that:

- the intended reader can identify the answer or main result quickly
- headings or section-opening sentences reveal the report's logical progression
- each major conclusion has sufficient, traceable evidence
- the report makes no stronger claim than the method supports
- visuals clarify a specific comparison and remain readable and accessible
- required venue or study-type reporting items are present or explicitly marked missing
- recommendations and limitations are specific rather than generic

Fix material failures before polishing language or layout. If a required evidence item is unavailable, state the gap and its effect on the conclusion.
